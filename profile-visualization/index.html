Legal structure

A Nation Run by Individuals, Not Institutions

Strip away the taxonomy and the geography, and the first thing the registry shows is who is actually doing business. Not corporations. Not cooperatives. People — filing under their own name, running an enterprise with no legal separation between themselves and the business.

Legal Structure of Formally-Registered Businesses

Excludes sole proprietorships — this shows only the shape of the formal remainder.

Partnerships are the largest formal category by far — nearly double every other formal structure combined. Share companies, the vehicle of choice for large capital-raising ventures, account for barely half a percent of all businesses in the country.

Who Owns These Businesses

What legal form these businesses take — individuals, or institutions.

Each bar is the share of that sector registered as a sole proprietorship; the pale track behind it is the remainder. The gradient is the point: formality rises steadily with capital intensity, from retail kiosks at the top to mining at the bottom. Hover for the full breakdown of legal forms.

The Formality Ladder

How much capital each legal form actually registers.

How to read: the dot is the median, the line spans the 25th to 75th percentile. The axis is logarithmic — each gridline is ten times the last — because the range runs from a few thousand birr to several million, which a normal axis would squash into one spike.

Businesses Against Capital

Where the businesses are is not where the money is.

Each sector has two shares: its share of the 345,369 registered businesses, and its share of the 2.1 trillion birr they collectively registered. The two come apart sharply. Logistics is a seventh of the register and well over half its capital; trade is half the register and a sixth of the capital.

Synthesis

One Registry, Two Economies

Put the three lenses together and the same shape appears from every angle. Ethiopia's registered economy in 2016 was overwhelmingly informal, overwhelmingly retail, and overwhelmingly concentrated in two regions — a vast field of small, individually-owned enterprises. Layered on top of that field sits a much smaller set of large, capital-intensive, often state-linked entities, visible mainly in the capital data, almost invisible in the count data.

Neither picture is the whole truth on its own. The count data says this is a nation of kiosks and minibuses; the capital data says a handful of corporations hold most of the money. Both are true at once — and the gap between them is, arguably, the single most useful thing this registry has to say about how the Ethiopian economy actually works.

What This Helps People Decide

Policy makers can identify regions or sectors where business support, infrastructure, or licensing services may be needed.

Investors and banks can compare market concentration and discover clusters of demand by sector and region.

Entrepreneurs and the public can understand where competition is dense and which business categories shape the formal economy.
